# Episode 1 — Mark Hanna

## Cold Open [00:00–01:30]
<!-- composition: 01-cold-open.html | first-word: The cartoon ran in every Hearst paper -->

The cartoon ran in every Hearst paper in eighteen ninety-six. A fat man in a suit covered in dollar signs, smoking a cigar the size of his forearm. The caption changed every week. The man did not.

His name was Mark Hanna.

He built the machine that bought American elections.

Mark Hanna was a Cleveland iron-and-coal millionaire. Fifty-nine years old. Had never run for office in his life.

It was the first campaign bought with corporate money at industrial scale.

Hanna raised three and a half million dollars that year. He spent twenty times what his opponent spent. He elected William McKinley, a wounded Civil War major and two-term Ohio governor, president of the United States. Banks paid. Railroads paid. Mark Hanna ran every dollar of it from a rented Manhattan office.

It was bought.

Standard Oil paid first. Carnegie Steel paid second. Banks and insurance companies paid in proportion to their capital. Hanna did not ask. He sent letters telling firms what they owed. No political consultant in American history has spent more, recruited more, or won bigger. Every campaign after eighteen ninety-six copied his playbook.

This is the man who built the operating system of American elections. This is the man nobody remembers.

Eight years later he was dead at sixty-six. The textbook revisions of the interwar decades took his name out of the standard curriculum. Today his Wikipedia article averages around six thousand views a month. Theodore Roosevelt's averages over three hundred thousand.

How one man bought a presidency. How the country he built forgot his name.

*How One Man Bought the Presidency in eighteen ninety-six*

## The Grocer's Son [01:45–04:15]
<!-- composition: 03-grocer-son.html | first-word: Marcus -->

Marcus Alonzo Hanna was born in eighteen thirty-seven in New Lisbon, Ohio. His father ran a country store. In eighteen fifty-three the family moved to Cleveland.

He went to Cleveland Central High School. One of his classmates was a quiet, devout, ferociously disciplined boy named John D. Rockefeller — the future founder of Standard Oil, who would in twenty years control nine of every ten gallons of refined American oil. They sat in the same room. They walked the same halls. Forty-three years later Rockefeller would write Hanna a check for two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.

Hanna left college after one semester at Western Reserve, the local Cleveland college two miles from his house, and went into his father's wholesale grocery business. By the end of the Civil War he ran the firm. Then he married into iron. His father-in-law owned one of the largest coal-and-iron operations on the Great Lakes — boats, docks, foundry contracts. Within a few years Hanna ran it. By eighteen eighty-five the company carried his name.

By eighteen eighty Hanna sat on the board of nine corporations. He owned the boats that moved iron ore down from the Lake Superior ranges, the docks where they unloaded, the railroad spurs that carried coal to the foundries. He had a private rail car. He had a winter house in Georgia. He had a city to himself.

By eighteen eighty, judging from the men he hired and the businesses he bought, Hanna had concluded that the federal government was being auctioned off in pieces — and that it could not last unless someone organized it from the top.

What he did not have, yet, was a candidate. And in the cornfields of Nebraska, a thirty-six-year-old populist lawyer named William Jennings Bryan was sharpening the speech that would, sixteen years later, force Hanna to invent the modern campaign in four months flat.

## The eighteen eighty-eight Moment [04:15–07:15]
<!-- composition: 04-1888-moment.html | first-word: The Republican National Convention opened in Chicago -->

The Republican National Convention opened in Chicago on June nineteenth, eighteen eighty-eight. Hanna arrived as a delegate from Ohio, working the floor for John Sherman, the Senator from his home state. Sherman was sixty-five, brilliant, deeply respected, and unable to win. Hanna knew it. He worked for him anyway.

The convention deadlocked. Through six ballots no candidate could break above three hundred votes. The favored son from Indiana, Benjamin Harrison — Civil War general, grandson of a former president, with no political constituency outside Indiana — hovered in second place. The party was looking for an alternative.

On the convention floor a delegation approached a forty-five-year-old congressman from Canton, Ohio, named William McKinley. They wanted him to allow his name to be placed in nomination. McKinley had come to Chicago pledged to John Sherman. He was Sherman's floor manager. He had been working all week for the man Hanna was working for.

McKinley said no. He said it without pausing to think about it. He said it without consulting his wife, his advisers, or his own ambition. He told the delegates that he was bound to Sherman by a public pledge and would not break it for the most powerful office in the country.

Hanna was watching from across the floor. He saw the entire exchange. He saw a man turn down the presidency of the United States because he had given his word to a colleague who could not win.

An industrialist remembers a man like that.

McKinley refused. Hanna remembered.

Harrison won the nomination on the eighth ballot and the presidency in November. Sherman went home. McKinley went back to his House seat.

Hanna went back to Cleveland with a decision in his pocket. The next Republican from Ohio who reached the White House would be William McKinley. And Hanna would put him there.

## Hanna Quits His Life [07:15–08:45]
<!-- composition: 05-hanna-quits.html | first-word: In January 1895 -->

In January eighteen ninety-five Hanna resigned from active management of M. A. Hanna & Company. He was fifty-seven years old. He turned the iron and coal operation over to his brother and his cousins. He took his name off the door in everything but title. He walked away from business.

He moved to New York City and rented offices in Manhattan. He set up a second headquarters in Chicago, under a young Ohio attorney named Charles Dawes. Dawes ran the field operation. Hanna ran the money.

He began making lists. Lists of bankers in New York. Lists of railroad presidents in Chicago. Lists of corporation heads from Pittsburgh to Saint Louis. He began making appointments. He began calling on people whose names had never appeared on a campaign ledger before.

He was building, in a Wall Street office at fifty-seven years old, the network he had wanted at thirty-five and had never been in a position to construct. He had four years to do it. He believed he had three.

The Republican National Convention opened in Saint Louis on June sixteenth, eighteen ninety-six. Two days later, on the first ballot, the convention nominated William McKinley for President of the United States. The vote was six hundred and sixty-one to 84. McKinley had been a candidate on paper for eleven days. He had been Hanna's candidate for eight years.

The Republican National Committee elected Hanna its Chairman on June 19. He now controlled every dollar the campaign would raise or spend between June and November. He had four months. He had a country to organize. And he had an idea about how to do it that no one had ever tried at scale.

## The Assessment [08:45–12:45]
<!-- composition: 06-assessment.html | first-word: The mechanism was simple -->

The mechanism was simple. And entirely unprecedented in American politics.

By July eighteen ninety-six, Hanna had decided that banks and corporations would be assessed for the campaign in proportion to their capital. He settled on one-quarter of one percent. A bank with twenty million dollars in capital owed fifty thousand. A railroad with two hundred million owed five hundred thousand. Small enough to feel like a tax. Large enough to fund a continent.

He did not call it a request. By every contemporary account, Hanna's approach was not a request. It was an assessment. The letter from Republican headquarters did not ask whether the firm wished to support the campaign. It told the firm what its share was. It named a date. It named an amount. It included payment instructions.

Standard Oil paid first. John D. Rockefeller paid first. Hanna's high-school classmate at Cleveland Central, forty-three years earlier, now in control of nine of every ten gallons of refined American oil.

He contributed two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. The equivalent of nine million dollars in modern money. The largest single political contribution in American history at the time. It came without a public announcement and without a public acknowledgment. Rockefeller paid. Hanna recorded the receipt. Both men understood what had been bought.

Carnegie Steel paid second. Other steel firms followed. Then the railroads: the Pennsylvania, the New York Central, the Baltimore and Ohio. Then the insurance houses: New York Life, Equitable, Mutual. Then the banks. By August every major financial institution in the Northeast had paid its assessment.

The total raised was at least three and a half million dollars. Some estimates run to ten million. Either figure dwarfed every previous American campaign. William Jennings Bryan, the Democratic nominee, raised somewhere between three hundred thousand and six hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars in the same period.

Hanna outspent him twenty to one.

The money was not collected for graft. Every dollar went into the campaign. Pamphlets, posters, train tickets, hotel bills, telegrams, surrogate speakers, mailings, billboards. Hanna ran a public-information operation, not a slush fund. The point was scale.

What Hanna had built was a financial machine. A system that connected the largest corporations in the country directly to the Republican National Committee through a recurring assessment, payable on demand, owed in proportion to size. It was a tax.

The companies paid because Hanna told them to. They paid because Hanna had been a businessman in Cleveland for thirty years and knew exactly which executive at which firm could authorize a check on which day.

And they paid because the alternative was Bryan winning.

Bryan wanted to coin silver alongside gold. The bankers ran the numbers. Under his plan, every dollar in every American bank would buy half as much by Inauguration Day. Every loan they had made would come back in cheaper money. The men who held the country's debts would become the men who absorbed the country's losses. For the people Hanna was writing to, that was not a policy disagreement. It was the end of their world.

After eighteen ninety-six every major American political party copied the assessment system. The names changed. The mechanism did not. But raising the money was the easy part. Hanna had four months to spend it.

## The Education of the Voters [12:45–15:15]
<!-- composition: 07-education.html | first-word: Hanna called the campaign -->

Hanna called the campaign an education.

Educating seventy-six million Americans in four months means industrial scale.

The Republican National Committee printed two hundred million pamphlets in the four months between June and November. Two hundred million. The American population in eighteen ninety-six was seventy-six million. Hanna mailed every adult voter the equivalent of four pamphlets each.

The literature was multilingual. He printed in German for the Pennsylvania and Wisconsin farmers. He printed in Yiddish for the New York garment workers. He printed in Spanish for the Southwest. He printed in Italian for the eastern cities. He printed in Polish, Czech, Swedish, and Norwegian for the upper Midwest.

The English-language pamphlets came in dozens of variants. One for railway workers. One for factory hands. One for shopkeepers. One for farmers in silver-mining states, and a different one for farmers in gold-standard states. The campaign tracked which version was sent to which county.

Hanna deployed fourteen hundred surrogate speakers. He paid their travel. He coordinated their schedules. He kept them on message. Every county in every battleground state heard a Republican voice in person before the November vote.

The candidate stayed home. McKinley refused to travel. He believed a presidential nominee should not beg for votes on a railroad platform. He told Hanna he would not campaign in the field. So Hanna brought the field to him.

The Pennsylvania Railroad, the Baltimore and Ohio, and a dozen other lines offered special excursion fares to Canton, Ohio — population thirty thousand, roughly three hundred miles from Washington, McKinley's home town since the Civil War. Voters arrived from as far as Maine and Nebraska. They walked from the train station to the candidate's front porch. McKinley spoke from his lawn. He shook every hand.

By Election Day, three quarters of a million Americans had stood in McKinley's front yard.

Bryan, meanwhile, traveled. The "Cross of Gold" speech he had delivered at the Democratic convention in Chicago on July ninth, eighteen ninety-six had made him the youngest major-party nominee in American history. The speech demanded the free coinage of silver to break what Bryan called the grip of Eastern banks on Western farmers. He closed it on a thesis: *"You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."* The convention floor went silent for five seconds, then erupted.

He spent the next four months on trains. He gave six hundred speeches in twenty-seven states. He was magnificent in person. He was thirty-six years old and could fill a stadium with his voice alone.

Hanna's response was tonnage, not eloquence. While Bryan thundered to crowds of ten thousand at a time, Hanna delivered two hundred million pieces of mail. While Bryan gave six hundred speeches, Hanna deployed fourteen hundred speakers. While Bryan held one rally per town, Hanna held one in every precinct. The contest was not between two campaigners. It was between a man and a machine.

On November third the country would tell them which it preferred.

## Election Night [15:15–16:45]
<!-- composition: 08-election-night.html | first-word: The polls closed -->

The polls closed in the East at six in the evening on November third, eighteen ninety-six. The first returns came in from Connecticut and New Hampshire — McKinley was running ahead of every Republican forecast. By midnight the Northeast had broken for him. By two in the morning the Midwest had followed. By dawn the only states still in question were in the silver-mining West.

McKinley took two hundred and seventy-one electoral votes. Bryan took one hundred and seventy-six. The popular vote was seven million, one hundred five thousand, seventy-six to six million, three hundred seventy thousand, eight hundred ninety-seven, a margin of more than seven hundred thousand. McKinley won by five point three percentage points. The total turnout — thirteen point nine million voters — was the highest, by share of eligible voters, in any American presidential election since the Civil War.

He took Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio by tens of thousands. He took New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania by hundreds of thousands. He swept every Northeastern state. He swept the Midwestern industrial belt. Bryan won the South, the Plains, and the silver-mining West, and lost everything else.

McKinley sent Hanna a telegram on November fifth. The text was meant for public release: "The people in their majesty, ignoring party lines, have declared their detestation of repudiation and dishonor. The victory is not to party or section, but of and for the whole American people."

The private letter that followed was different. McKinley wrote to Hanna alone: "Was there ever such unselfish devotion before? Your unfaltering and increasing friendship through more than twenty years has been to me an encouragement and a source of strength which I am sure you have never realized, but which I have constantly felt and for which I thank you from the bottom of my heart."

Twenty years. They had met in eighteen seventy-six. McKinley had been a young congressman, Hanna a young industrialist looking for a candidate. The eighteen eighty-eight convention had been twelve years into the friendship. The eighteen ninety-six campaign had been the eighth year of the project. McKinley remembered the math. He thanked Hanna for two decades.

## The Waldorf Dinner [16:45–20:15]
<!-- composition: 09-waldorf-dinner.html | first-word: Four nights after the election -->

Four nights after the election, on November seventh, eighteen ninety-six, Mark Hanna hosted a private dinner at the Hotel Waldorf in New York. He invited thirty-one men — every newspaper reporter who had covered Republican headquarters in New York and Chicago from June to November.

He did not invite only the friendly press. Bryan's papers were there. Hearst's *New York Journal* was there — the paper whose Davenport cartoons had spent the campaign drawing Hanna in his dollar-sign suit. Republican and Democratic correspondents ate the same meal and received the same gift.

The gift was a medal the size and weight of a twenty-dollar gold piece. On one side was the reporter's own name, engraved. On the other side were the words "McKinley and Hobart — Sound Money and Protection." A small gold eagle with outstretched wings was set above the inscription. Each medal was struck individually. Hanna handed them around the table himself, calling each man by name.

The Vienna Progress, a small Georgia weekly, ran the full account a week later.

When he rose to speak, Hanna did not toast McKinley. He toasted the press.

"When I began this campaign," he said, "I felt that I should be safe if I only had the friendship of the newspaper men. I began to diagnose the situation. Now that the situation has been brought to a successful issue, I don't mind telling you I picked up a great many pointers from you. I found that every one of you were just as fully alive to the situation as I was. ... They came to me fresh from the people whom they had seen, and, I must say, I got as much information from them as they did from me." <!-- noqa: STYLE: verbatim primary-source quote, Vienna Progress, Nov 12 1896, see research.md §2.1 -->

He was telling thirty-one reporters, four days after winning the presidency, that he had used them as an intelligence network. He had read their dispatches every morning since June. He had tracked which states were drifting and which were holding. He had adjusted his pamphlet runs and his surrogate routes by what the press observed in the counties. The reporters were not propaganda targets. They were sources.

Polling did not yet exist. Focus groups did not yet exist. The reporters were how he saw the country.

He sat down. Each reporter went home with a gold medal. By morning the story was on every wire service in the country, and Mark Hanna had become the most powerful unelected man in America.

## Senator Hanna [20:15–21:45]
<!-- composition: 10-senator-hanna.html | first-word: John Sherman resigned -->

John Sherman resigned his seat in the United States Senate in early eighteen ninety-seven to become William McKinley's Secretary of State. The resignation cleared the most coveted political position in Ohio at exactly the moment Mark Hanna needed one. On March fifth, eighteen ninety-seven, Ohio's Republican governor, no friend of Hanna's, in office because Hanna had not opposed him, appointed Hanna to fill the seat.

The appointment was widely understood as a payoff. Hanna had spent twenty years putting McKinley in the White House. The Senate seat was the receipt.

In January eighteen ninety-eight the Ohio General Assembly elected Hanna to a full six-year term. The election was contested. An Ohio state senator testified under oath that Hanna's people had offered him cash to flip his vote. The U.S. Senate investigated and declined to sustain the charges. Hanna kept the seat. The story stayed in the papers for two years.

He served from March fifth, eighteen ninety-seven, to February fifteenth, nineteen oh four. He never lost an election and never lost a vote. He chaired the Republican National Committee through both McKinley campaigns and into Roosevelt's first term. For six of those seven years he was the most powerful unelected man in America. For the seventh, he was the second most powerful.

## The Madman [21:45–23:45]
<!-- composition: 11-the-madman.html | first-word: Don't any of you realize -->

"Don't any of you realize there's only one life between this madman and the presidency?"

Mark Hanna said it on the convention floor in Philadelphia, June twenty-first, nineteen hundred. He said it loud enough to be heard across the room. Reporters quoted it that afternoon. By the end of the week the line had reached every major paper in the country.

The madman in question was Theodore Roosevelt.

The trouble had started in eighteen ninety-nine. Vice President Garret Hobart — Hanna's closest ally inside the McKinley administration, the man Hanna had personally selected for the eighteen ninety-six ticket — fell ill in the autumn and died on November twenty-first, eighteen ninety-nine. McKinley was seeking a second term. The vice presidency was open.

The New York Republican machine, run by Senator Thomas Platt, had been trying for two years to dispose of the state's reform governor: a forty-one-year-old reformer named Theodore Roosevelt. Rough Rider in the Spanish-American War, former Police Commissioner of New York City, the loudest voice for civil-service reform in the Republican Party.

Roosevelt had spent his term breaking up the corrupt patronage system that kept Platt's organization alive. Platt wanted him gone. The vice presidency was a gilded coffin. Platt began maneuvering to make it Roosevelt's.

Hanna was horrified. He had spent four years building a relationship with McKinley that defined American politics. The vice president was one bullet from the presidency. And Hanna did not trust Roosevelt — not because Roosevelt was a bad man, but because Roosevelt was a moralist. Hanna's politics were transactional. Roosevelt did not do deals. He picked fights and refused to fund them. He would inherit the corporate alliances that funded the party — and break them.

The Republican National Convention opened in Philadelphia on June nineteenth, nineteen hundred. Roosevelt arrived as the Western delegations' favorite. Platt had already pledged New York. Pennsylvania followed. By the second day Roosevelt had momentum. The nomination was going to happen.

Hanna stood on the Philadelphia floor watching the votes accumulate. He had built the Republican Party's machinery. He chaired its committee. And he could not stop the convention from nominating, for vice president of the United States, a man he believed would destroy everything the party stood for.

That was when he said it. The line that would follow him for the rest of his life.

The convention nominated Roosevelt for vice president on June twenty-first, nineteen hundred. McKinley accepted. Hanna campaigned for the ticket anyway. McKinley and Roosevelt won the November election by a wider margin than McKinley and Hobart had won in eighteen ninety-six.

Hanna was right. There was only one life between Roosevelt and the presidency. It was William McKinley's.

## The Cowboy Takes Over [23:45–25:45]
<!-- composition: 12-cowboy.html | first-word: On September 6th -->

On September sixth, nineteen oh one, William McKinley stood in a receiving line at the Temple of Music at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York. He was shaking hands with members of the public.

The line had been moving for ten minutes when a twenty-eight-year-old former steelworker named Leon Czolgosz approached the President with a handkerchief wrapped around his right hand. American-born, son of Polish immigrants. Out of work since the eighteen ninety-three economic crash. Radicalized the year before by the anarchist lecture circuit. The handkerchief concealed a .32 caliber Iver Johnson revolver.

Czolgosz fired twice. The first bullet glanced off McKinley's coat button. The second entered his abdomen.

...

The President was carried to the home of a Buffalo lawyer who had personally invited McKinley to come, on Delaware Avenue. The doctors operated. They could not find the bullet. They closed the wound and waited. For a week McKinley appeared to be recovering.

On September thirteenth his condition deteriorated.

He died at 2:15 in the morning on September fourteenth, nineteen oh one.

...

Vice President Roosevelt was hiking on Mount Marcy in the Adirondacks when McKinley was shot. A guide located him. A buckboard and a special train carried him to Buffalo. He took the oath of office at the Wilcox House on Delaware Avenue at three thirty in the afternoon on September 14, hours after McKinley's death.

Hanna was in Cleveland. When the news reached him that McKinley had died and Roosevelt had been sworn in, he gave the response that would follow him for the rest of his life. He said it to a friend, in a private room, with no reporter present. The remark traveled through political channels until it reached the public record:

"Now we've got that damned cowboy as President!"

...

The line was Hanna's epitaph. It was also a forecast.

## The Ohio Trap [25:45–28:45]
<!-- composition: 13-ohio-trap.html | first-word: two and a half years left -->

Hanna was sixty-four years old when McKinley died. He had two and a half years left, though no one knew that yet. He spent them in a slow-motion confrontation with the man he had not been able to stop in Philadelphia.

Roosevelt and Hanna agreed on almost nothing. Roosevelt wanted to break up the trusts. Hanna had built his fortune on them. Roosevelt wanted to regulate the railroads. Hanna sat on the boards of three of them. Roosevelt wanted to give labor a seat at the table. Hanna had spent forty years keeping it off. The two men shared a party, a Cabinet, and almost nothing else.

In the autumn of nineteen oh three, with the nineteen oh four nomination a year away, Hanna began quietly weighing whether to challenge Roosevelt for the Republican nomination. He never said so publicly. He never built an organization. He told confidants he was thinking about it. He let the corporate donors who had funded McKinley know he was available. He did not announce.

Roosevelt and his ally Joseph Foraker — two-term Ohio governor, Senator since eighteen ninety-seven, the man Hanna had blocked from a Cabinet seat — knew. They set a trap.

The Ohio Republican State Convention met in Columbus in October nineteen oh three. A resolution was introduced from the floor endorsing President Roosevelt's renomination in nineteen oh four. The resolution was a year early. State conventions did not normally endorse presidential nominees until the spring of an election year. The timing was deliberate. It was meant to force Hanna's hand.

Hanna did what any operator of his experience would do: he went to Roosevelt directly. He told the President he might oppose the resolution as premature. He framed it as a procedural objection, not a political one. He gave Roosevelt the chance to back off.

Roosevelt's reply, sent by letter, was one of the most elegant political knives in American history. He was not requesting Hanna's support, he wrote. He was not requesting anyone's support. But he was confident that all *loyal supporters of his administration* would naturally vote for the resolution. The decision, of course, was Hanna's.

Voting against the resolution would have been a public answer to the question.

Hanna voted yes. He had no choice. The Ohio convention passed the endorsement unanimously. The nineteen oh four nomination was settled. Hanna's challenge ended before it began.

He had been outplayed by a man he had publicly called a madman. Outplayed without a single dollar being raised, a single delegate being courted, or a single overt threat being made.

...

Four months later he was dead.

## The Death [28:45–30:45]
<!-- composition: 14-the-death.html | first-word: In early February 1904 -->

In early February nineteen oh four Mark Hanna fell ill at the Arlington Hotel in Washington, D.C. — two blocks from the White House, the city's most fashionable address, the place where senators stayed when they did not keep a Washington home.

He was running a fever. The Senate was in session. He was working from his hotel room. The diagnosis came back: typhoid fever. A disease of contaminated water and poor sanitation, common in the city's poorer neighborhoods, less common in its hotels.

The Theodore Roosevelt Center holds the medical bulletin. It is dated February twelfth, nineteen oh four. The bulletin reports active illness. By February fourteenth he could not sit up.

Hanna died at six forty in the evening on February fifteenth, nineteen oh four.

...

He was sixty-six years old. He had served in the United States Senate for six years and eleven months. He had chaired the Republican National Committee for eight. He had run a Cleveland business for forty.

His body was returned to Cleveland by funeral train. He was buried at Lake View Cemetery, on the same hilltop where he had helped arrange the burial of James Garfield twenty-three years earlier. Twenty thousand people attended.

Roosevelt did not come. He sent his Cabinet.

Four days after the funeral, on February nineteenth, nineteen oh four, Theodore Roosevelt wrote a letter to his son Ted at Groton:

"Poor Hanna's death was a tragedy. At the end he wrote me a note, the last he ever wrote, which showed him at his best, and which I much appreciate. His death was very sad for his family and close friends, for he had many large and generous traits, and had made a great success in life by his energy, perseverance and burly strength."

Roosevelt could afford to be generous. He had won.

## nineteen oh seven: The Tillman Act [30:45–32:00]
<!-- composition: 15-tillman-act.html | first-word: On January 26 1907 -->

On January twenty-sixth, nineteen oh seven, Theodore Roosevelt signed the Tillman Act into law.

...

The bill had been introduced by Senator Benjamin Tillman of South Carolina — a former governor, an outspoken white-supremacist demagogue, and one of the few sitting senators willing to publicly attack Wall Street. He was a Roosevelt ally on this bill alone. It made it a federal crime for any national bank or any corporation organized under federal law to contribute money to any political campaign.

The exact mechanism Hanna had pioneered in eighteen ninety-six — the assessment letter to corporate treasurers, demanding payment proportional to capital — became a federal felony eleven years after he invented it. Standard Oil could not legally pay another assessment. Carnegie Steel could not. The railroads could not. The insurance companies could not. The system that had elected McKinley twice was now a crime.

Roosevelt did not name Hanna in the signing statement. He did not need to. Every newspaper in the country understood what the bill outlawed. The Tillman Act became the most consequential campaign-finance reform in American history until the Federal Election Campaign Act of nineteen seventy-one. It stayed on the books, in some form, for one hundred and three years.

## twenty ten: Citizens United [32:00–33:15]
<!-- composition: 16-citizens-united.html | first-word: On January 21 2010 -->

On January twenty-first, twenty ten, the Supreme Court of the United States decided *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission* by a vote of five to four. The opinion held that corporate political expenditures, when made independently of a candidate's campaign, are protected speech under the First Amendment.

The Tillman Act's federal ban on corporate political spending was effectively gutted. The wall Roosevelt had built around Hanna's system in nineteen oh seven came down in twenty ten. Within five years of the Citizens United decision, independent corporate political spending exceeded one billion dollars per election cycle. By twenty twenty-four, corporate-funded Super PACs had raised more than four billion dollars in a single election.

Hanna's system was back. In a different legal form. But back.

The court did not cite Mark Hanna. The plaintiffs did not invoke him. The dissents did not name him. Hanna had been dead for one hundred and six years, and the system he built was being relegalized in a courtroom four blocks from the Senate floor where he had served his only term. The country had forgotten the man whose machinery it was rebuilding.

## Why You've Never Heard His Name [33:15–35:00]
<!-- composition: 17-why-forgotten.html | first-word: Three things erased him -->

Mark Hanna died in nineteen oh four. The Davenport image — Hanna in the dollar-sign suit, cigar the size of a forearm — kept circulating for years through reprints, Bryan campaign materials, and the eighteen ninety-eight collected edition Davenport published before moving from the *Journal* to the rival *Evening Mail*. By the early 1920s it was gone.

Three things erased him. They did it slowly. They did it without anyone noticing.

The first was Roosevelt's Tillman Act. After January nineteen oh seven Hanna's methods were a federal crime. No Republican Party leader could celebrate the man who invented the practice the party's own president had just outlawed. Hanna's name became politically toxic to the people who had benefited most from his work. The party that owed him everything had every reason to stop mentioning him. So it stopped.

The second was the textbook revisions across the interwar decades. American high schools standardized their history curricula in the years after the First World War. The Gilded Age chapter tightened. The Progressive Era expanded. The story became Roosevelt versus the trusts, Wilson versus the banks, the muckrakers versus the bosses.

Hanna did not fit. He was not a clean villain. McKinley's family liked him. The press corps respected him. His Senate colleagues mourned him.

In his last years, he co-founded the National Civic Federation, an experiment in putting corporate executives and union leaders at the same table. He was the only Gilded Age industrialist who would publicly share a stage with the head of the largest American union of the era.

And he was not a hero either. He was the mechanism. By the 1930s the new textbooks had dropped him. A generation of American students learned the eighteen ninety-six campaign without learning the name of the man who ran it.

The third is the one you can measure in twenty twenty-six. Hanna's Wikipedia article averages around six thousand views a month. McKinley's averages a hundred and forty-five thousand. Theodore Roosevelt's averages three hundred and twenty-four thousand. John D. Rockefeller's, the man who paid Hanna's first assessment in eighteen ninety-six, averages a hundred and fifty-one thousand.

Hanna gets one fiftieth of Roosevelt's traffic. He gets one twenty-fifth of Rockefeller's. The other names from the eighteen ninety-six campaign are fifty times more interesting to the present-day reader than the man who built the campaign.

Americans search for what Hanna built. They do not search for him. The erasure that started with Roosevelt's reforms and continued through the textbook revisions of the interwar decades is still active. It is still working. The reason you have never heard his name is that for one hundred and twenty years the people who knew it had reasons not to say it.

...

In eighteen ninety-six the cartoon was a joke. Mark Hanna in a suit covered in dollar signs, smoking a cigar the size of a forearm — every week, every Hearst paper, the same man. The joke was that anyone could see what Hanna was doing.

The joke was right.

He raised the money. He elected the president. He invented modern campaign finance. The system the cartoon was mocking became the way American elections work. The cartoon stopped running. The system did not.

This is the man nobody remembers.
