# Mark Hanna — Full-Scale Documentary Script
## "The Ledger That Won America"

> Working title. Final title to be locked at edit.
> Target runtime: 22:00 (±0:30).
> Register: HBO investigative documentary. Ken Burns archival × Adam Curtis cold-eyed political history. Period-correct, dry, and structurally damning.
> Reference baseline: the 4-minute preview at `renders/mark-hanna-ep01-preview.mp4`. Script is engineered for **engagement beats** — every 90–150 seconds, a delivery moment lands at the point an attentive viewer would otherwise drift.
> Voice constraints baked in: per `LEARNINGS.md`, all years spelled as words ("eighteen ninety-six"), no `Word, descriptor` openers, no comma-listed nouns that blur into proper names, JP Morgan written without periods, no reliance on punctuation for pacing.

---

## 1. Logline

In eighteen ninety-six, a Cleveland coal-and-iron broker named Mark Hanna spent three and a half million dollars to install a president — and quietly invented the operating system every American campaign has run on for the next century. This is the story of the man who turned democracy into a balance sheet, the candidate he made, the preacher he beat, the cowboy he couldn't control, and the cartoon that outlived him.

---

## 2. Theme statement

Power is not the loudest thing in the room. Power is the thing that decides where the money goes. The documentary is not about money or politics — it is about the moment those two stopped being separate.

---

## 3. Character bios (each must carry narrative load — no filler)

Every named character serves a structural purpose. If a character can be removed without collapsing the spine, they are removed.

### 3.1 MARK HANNA — the protagonist
The inventor. Not a candidate, not an ideologue, not a believer. A merchant who recognized that politics was an unsupervised market. Hanna is the **mechanism** of the story; the camera lives behind his eyes for most of the runtime.

**Narrative function.** Hanna is the question the documentary keeps asking and never quite answers: was he a corruption or a clarification? Each act gives the viewer a different answer and refuses to choose.

### 3.2 WILLIAM McKINLEY — the instrument
The candidate. A 45-year-old congressman from Canton, Ohio, with a soft voice and a private bankruptcy. Loyal — extinct-level loyal in a transactional era. That loyalty is what made him purchasable, in the way that only the genuinely incorruptible can be purchased.

**Narrative function.** McKinley is what Hanna's money buys. Every time the documentary returns to his face, the viewer should feel the strange softness of the human being on the receiving end of the machine. He is the moral question the audience carries.

### 3.3 DANIEL RHODES — the gateway
Hanna's father-in-law. Coal and iron. Cleveland old money. The man who hands Hanna the keys to the Lake Erie chokepoint and, with it, the leverage that becomes everything.

**Narrative function.** Rhodes appears once, in act two, and never again. He is the **inheritance moment** — the door Hanna walks through. The audience must understand that Hanna did not start at zero and did not pretend to.

### 3.4 JOHN D. ROCKEFELLER — the mirror
The classmate. The other Cleveland boy. The man who chose to bend a single industry to his will instead of bending a country. Rockefeller stays in private power; Hanna goes public. They orbit each other for forty years and never collide.

**Narrative function.** Rockefeller is the **road not taken**. Every time the documentary cuts to him, the viewer is reminded that Hanna had a choice — that political power was not the only kind on offer. He is the negative space that defines the shape of Hanna's ambition.

### 3.5 WILLIAM JENNINGS BRYAN — the threat
The preacher. Thirty-six years old in eighteen ninety-six, with the best speaking voice in American politics and a moral certainty that terrified the bond market. The Cross of Gold. The Boy Orator of the Platte. The man who came closer to breaking the gold standard than any major-party candidate before or since.

**Narrative function.** Bryan is the **human counter-argument**. The documentary must make the audience feel that he could have won — that a country could have chosen him. Without Bryan, Hanna's ledger is just bookkeeping. With Bryan, the ledger is the thing that defeated something real.

### 3.6 THEODORE ROOSEVELT — the antithesis
The cowboy. Thirty-eight years old in eighteen ninety-six. War hero by appetite. The next thing. Hanna saw him coming and did not believe what he was seeing.

**Narrative function.** Roosevelt is the **system error**. Hanna's machine is built on the assumption that politicians are buyable, predictable, manageable. Roosevelt is none of those things. His arrival is the documentary's structural irony: the man who built the operating system is destroyed by the first user who refuses to read the manual.

### 3.7 CHARLES DAWES — the apprentice (minor)
The lieutenant. Hanna's man on the ground in Chicago through the campaign. Later a vice president, a Nobel laureate, a banker. Dawes appears briefly and exists for one structural reason.

**Narrative function.** Dawes proves the **method replicates**. Hanna's death does not end Hanna's system. The audience needs to see, however briefly, that the ledger could be passed on.

### ACT I — THE COLD OPEN (0:00 – 4:00)

The reference render is the spine of this act. Treat it as locked; the surgical work is to extend it.

#### Scene 1 — "The Cartoon" (0:00 – 1:00)

**Setting.** Newsroom paper-wall: stacked Hearst political cartoons. Ink Black background. Single sustained low drone (no melody yet — period silence).
**Characters present.** Hanna (in cartoon form only).
**Intended emotion.** Recognition without identification. The viewer feels they have seen this face but never been told the name.

**HF.** Cold open: pinned cartoon montage. Three Davenport / Hearst cartoons crossfade across two seconds each. No camera motion until the third image, which slow-pushes 1.0 → 1.04 over the final beat. Vignette begins to close.

**VO** (George, dry, no music):
> The cartoon ran in every Hearst paper in America.
> A fat man in a suit covered in dollar signs, smoking a cigar the size of his forearm.
> The caption changed every week. The man did not.
> His name was Mark Hanna.

**TEXT** (Playfair Display 900, Newsprint Cream, lower-third, fades on with the name): `MARK HANNA`. Below in IBM Plex Mono Faded Bone: `1837 – 1904`.

#### Scene 2 — "Eighteen Ninety-Six" (1:00 – 2:15)

**Setting.** Number theatre. Black field. Numerals fill frame.
**Characters present.** Hanna offscreen; the system is the character.
**Intended emotion.** Inarguable scale. The viewer is being shown the size of the thing before the thing is named.

**HF.** Year drop: `1896` in Blood Oxide IBM Plex Mono fills frame at 480 px, expo.out 800 ms. Hold three seconds. Hard cut to count-up: `$3,500,000` builds digit-by-digit in Hanna Amber over 1.2 s. On the spoken word *twenty*, a Blood Oxide stamp `20×` slams in (180 px, scale 1.2 → 1.0 in 120 ms).

**VO:**
> And in eighteen ninety-six, he did something no one had ever done at that scale.
> He turned the American presidential election into a corporate purchase order.
> Three and a half million dollars raised. Twenty times what his opponent spent.
> Standard Oil, two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.
> JP Morgan, two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.
> Half a million voters, transported by train, to shake one man's hand.
> Two hundred million pieces of campaign literature, printed and mailed.
> The opposition had a thirty-six year old preacher who could move a crowd to tears.
> Hanna had a ledger.
> And when November came, the ledger won.

**TEXT** (stacked ledger lines, IBM Plex Mono, build under VO):
```
STANDARD OIL  ............  $250,000
JP MORGAN     ............  $250,000
RAILROADS     ............    [blank]
BANKS         ............    0.25%
```
The recipient line is **left blank** until the final beat, when `MCKINLEY` types in over 600 ms in Newsprint Cream.

#### Scene 3 — Cold-open close (2:15 – 4:00)

**Setting.** Title card. Black field. Single shaft of cream light.
**Characters present.** None on-screen.
**Intended emotion.** The cold open's bell. The viewer should feel the documentary has just made a contract with them.

**HF.** Audio drops to room tone for 1.5 seconds. Single low piano note. Title types in over 1.4 seconds: `THE LEDGER`. Subtitle in IBM Plex Mono Faded Bone: `An American Investment in the Presidency`.

**VO** (after silence, quietly):
> He was not born with money.
> He was born in a grocery.

[Hard cut to black. Three seconds of silence. Title card fades up.]

### ACT II — THE EDUCATION (4:00 – 11:00)

How a country grocer's son became the man who could write a check that rewrote a country.

#### Scene 4 — "New Lisbon, Ohio" (4:00 – 5:30)

**Setting.** Period photograph: a frame storefront on a muddy main street. Period sepia. Slow Ken Burns push-in.
**Characters present.** Young Mark Hanna (still photograph, age fifteen). His father, Doctor Leonard Hanna (referenced).
**Intended emotion.** A working-class childhood. The camera makes the viewer feel that this was a small life that became enormous.

**HF.** Slow push-in over a single archival photograph. 12 seconds. Vignette stays heavy. No on-screen text for the first 8 seconds — voice and image only.

**VO:**
> New Lisbon, Ohio. Eighteen thirty-seven.
> His father, Doctor Leonard Hanna, was a country physician who ran a wholesale grocery on the side.
> Flour and salt and dry goods to Ohio farmers.
> When Mark was fifteen, the family moved to Cleveland.

**TEXT** (Plex Mono, Faded Bone, lower-right, fades at the eight-second mark): `Cleveland — population ~17,000. Lake Erie port. Ore depot for the Pittsburgh mills.`

**HF** (transition out): cross-dissolve to the Western Reserve College yearbook page. Two faces side by side.

#### Scene 5 — "The Classmate" (5:30 – 7:00)

**Setting.** A single yearbook page. Two seventeen-year-old faces.
**Characters present.** Hanna (young). Rockefeller (young, on the same page).
**Intended emotion.** Foreshadow. The audience is told a fact and immediately understands the weight of it.

**HF.** Both portraits fade up together. As the VO names Rockefeller, his portrait grows brighter while Hanna's recedes 30 percent — but does not vanish. The two faces stay co-resident.

**VO:**
> He enrolled at Western Reserve College.
> In his class was a quiet, awkward boy who would grow up to control ninety percent of the oil refining capacity in the United States.
> His name was John D. Rockefeller.
> The two would orbit each other for the next forty years.

**TEXT** (lower-third, after the name lands): `John D. Rockefeller — Standard Oil Company, 1870 –`

#### Scene 6 — "The Real Education" (7:00 – 8:00)

**Setting.** Inside the Cleveland grocery, photographed in period halftone. Light from one window. A ledger on a table.
**Characters present.** Hanna at twenty. Daniel Rhodes implied, off-camera.
**Intended emotion.** The viewer should feel the moment a young man learns the texture of leverage.

**HF.** Slow Ken Burns over the ledger photograph. Numbers in the ledger are subtly highlighted in Hanna Amber as the VO names what he was watching for.

**VO:**
> Hanna lasted one year at Western Reserve before he quit.
> Not because he was failing.
> Because he had already figured out something the professors could not teach.
> The real education was in the price of things.
> He went back to the store. Worked the floor.
> Watched how men negotiated.
> Watched who paid on time and who did not.
> Watched which suppliers had leverage, and which ones begged.

**TEXT** (a single line types in at 7:55, Source Serif 4 italic): *"He learned to read a man the way other men read a balance sheet."*

#### Scene 7 — "Daniel Rhodes" (8:00 – 9:00)

**Setting.** Period portrait of Daniel Rhodes. Grey beard. Heavy frock coat. Lake Erie schooners in the matted background.
**Characters present.** Daniel Rhodes (the gateway). Charlotte Rhodes (named). Hanna (referenced).
**Intended emotion.** The door opens. The audience understands that Hanna did not bootstrap into wealth — he married into the **chokepoint**.

**HF.** Two-image composition. Left: Charlotte Rhodes, sepia portrait, soft. Right: Daniel Rhodes, harder light, eyes forward. Between them, a hairline rule in Hanna Amber. As the VO names "coal and iron," a third image fades in below: a Lake Erie ore boat unloading at a Cleveland dock.

**VO:**
> Then the Civil War came. He served briefly. Came home.
> And married a woman named Charlotte Rhodes.
> Her father was Daniel Rhodes.
> Coal and iron.
> One of the men quietly getting rich off everything Cleveland was becoming.
> Hanna walked into that business, and never walked out.

**TEXT** (Plex Mono, lower-right, three lines build under VO):
```
1864 — marries Charlotte Rhodes
1867 — partner, Rhodes & Co.
1885 — full ownership, M.A. Hanna & Company
```

#### Scene 8 — "The Chokepoint" (9:00 – 10:30)

**Setting.** Animated map of the Great Lakes. Iron ore moves from the Mesabi Range to Cleveland to Pittsburgh. Hanna Amber arrows.
**Characters present.** Hanna as a name on a building, not a face.
**Intended emotion.** Geography is destiny. The viewer should feel the leverage in a physical sense.

**HF.** Map animation. Period parchment texture. Arrows travel with the VO; on the word *chokepoint*, every arrow narrows through a single point on Cleveland's lake front. Slow zoom-in to the Cleveland warehouse district. Final pull-out to the city skyline silhouette.

**VO:**
> By eighteen sixty-seven, Marcus Alonzo Hanna had built MA Hanna and Company.
> A coal and iron brokerage, sitting at the exact pivot point where Lake Erie ore boats unloaded for the Pittsburgh steel mills.
> Cleveland was the chokepoint. And Hanna sat on it.
> Every ton of iron ore that crossed Lake Erie in the eighteen seventies passed through brokers like him, and he took a cut.
> Then he bought the newspaper, the Cleveland Herald.
> Then a theater. Then banking interests. Then a streetcar line.
> By eighteen eighty, he controlled enough of Cleveland that when he walked into a room, the room changed temperature.

**TEXT** (single anchor line at end, Playfair Display 700, Newsprint Cream, 60 px): **The room changed temperature.**

#### Scene 9 — "The Boredom" (10:30 – 11:00)

**Setting.** Hanna's office. Sepia interior. A wall of ledgers. A man at a desk, back to camera.
**Characters present.** Hanna (silhouette only).
**Intended emotion.** The pivot of the whole documentary. Power has reached its ceiling and is asking what is next.

**HF.** Single locked-off photograph. Subtle parallax — the foreground desk drifts forward 1.02 over fifteen seconds while the background ledger wall stays static. Sound design drops the music entirely.

**VO** (slower, quieter, almost inward):
> And he was bored.
> Not with money. He had money.
> Bored with the ceiling.
> Because Hanna had figured out, watching the men who supplied his coal, the politicians who regulated his railroads, the judges who settled his contracts — that money was the second most powerful thing in America.
> The most powerful thing was who decided where the money went.
> That was politics.
> And no one had ever run it like a business.
> Not yet.

[Hard cut to black. Two seconds of silence.]

### ACT III — THE CANDIDATE (11:00 – 17:00)

The discovery, the test run, the man, the threat, the campaign that rewrote the playbook.

#### Scene 10 — Title card "THE CANDIDATE" (11:00 – 11:15)

**HF.** Cream-on-black title types in over 1.2 s. Subtitle in Plex Mono Faded Bone: `1888 – 1896`. Hold three seconds. Cross-dissolve to convention floor.

#### Scene 11 — "The Convention Floor" (11:15 – 12:30)

**Setting.** Chicago, eighteen eighty-eight. Republican National Convention. Period photograph of the convention hall, packed, smoke-thick.
**Characters present.** Hanna (working the floor). John Sherman (the failed candidate). McKinley (introduced).
**Intended emotion.** Backroom mechanics. The audience watches a craft being practiced.

**HF.** Slow vertical pan up the convention hall photograph from floor to gallery. As the VO names *delegates*, *postmasterships*, *markers*, three small graphics overlay the crowd: each names a thing being traded with a hairline arrow.

**VO:**
> The year was eighteen eighty-eight.
> The Republican National Convention. Chicago.
> Mark Hanna arrived not as a candidate, but as a kingmaker.
> His man was John Sherman. Ohio senator. Treasury veteran. The establishment choice.
> Hanna worked the convention floor for six days.
> Trading delegates. Promising postmasterships. Calling in markers from Ohio to Pennsylvania.
> Sherman peaked on the first ballot at two hundred and twenty-nine votes.
> Then he bled out. Ballot after ballot, until the nomination went to Benjamin Harrison on the eighth.
> Hanna lost.

**TEXT** (vote-tally graphic builds across right edge, Plex Mono):
```
BALLOT 1 — Sherman 229
BALLOT 2 — Sherman 249
BALLOT 3 — Sherman 244
...
BALLOT 8 — Harrison 544 ✓
```

#### Scene 12 — "Finding His President" (12:30 – 13:30)

**Setting.** Same convention floor. Slow push-in on a single, smaller figure — McKinley at the resolutions committee.
**Characters present.** McKinley (introduced). Hanna (watching).
**Intended emotion.** The moment a hunter finds his rifle. The audience should feel that something has just been recognized.

**HF.** Spot-light effect: convention hall fades to grey except for one figure, who holds full sepia color. Slow push-in to a quarter-frame portrait. The figure walks to the microphone. The microphone is the last thing in focus.

**VO:**
> But he saw something that week he would never forget.
> A forty-five year old congressman from Canton, Ohio had been chairing the resolutions committee.
> When delegates began breaking toward him as a dark-horse alternative, the congressman walked to the microphone, and refused.
> He told the convention he was bound to Sherman. And he sat down.
> That kind of loyalty was rare in eighteen eighty-eight politics. It was almost extinct.
> His name was William McKinley.
> Hanna walked across the floor, and introduced himself.
> He did not know it yet.
> But he had just found his president.

**TEXT** (lower-third, Playfair Display 900, on the name): `WILLIAM McKINLEY`. Below in Plex Mono: `Congressman, Ohio 17th district. Loyalty rating: extinct.`

#### Scene 13 — "The Bankruptcy" (13:30 – 14:30)

**Setting.** Eighteen ninety-three. The Panic. A piece of paper on a desk: a personal note for a hundred and thirty thousand dollars, McKinley's signature on it, a friend's name above his.
**Characters present.** McKinley. Robert Walker (named, the friend who took him down). Hanna (off-camera).
**Intended emotion.** Twist. The audience expected a politician; they get a man.

**HF.** Hard cut to a close-up of a period promissory note. Camera rises slowly. As the VO reaches *one hundred and thirty thousand dollars*, the figure burns onto the page in Blood Oxide. Vignette closes around the signature.

**VO:**
> Five years later, Hanna would prove how much the loyalty had been worth.
> In eighteen ninety-three, William McKinley discovered he had cosigned notes for a friend named Robert Walker.
> The friend went bankrupt.
> McKinley owed one hundred and thirty thousand dollars.
> He did not have one hundred and thirty thousand dollars.
> He had a governor's salary in Ohio and a wife who needed full-time care.
> He prepared to resign in disgrace.

[Pause. Single low piano note.]

> Hanna made one phone call.
> Inside seventy-two hours, a syndicate of Cleveland industrialists had bought the notes outright.
> No interest. No repayment schedule. No public announcement.
> McKinley kept the governorship.
> And Hanna had not bought a politician.
> He had bought the only kind of politician who would feel the debt for the rest of his life — the honest one.

**TEXT** (after the closing line, italicized): *"The most expensive thing you can buy in politics is a man who did not know he was for sale."*

#### Scene 14 — "Cross of Gold" (14:30 – 15:30)

**Setting.** Chicago, July ninth, eighteen ninety-six. Democratic National Convention. The Coliseum. Twenty thousand people. One man at a podium.
**Characters present.** William Jennings Bryan.
**Intended emotion.** Counter-reveal. Until now the documentary has been about Hanna. For the next sixty seconds it is not. The threat has a face.

**HF.** Hard pivot in visual language. Switch from sepia to a colder, bluer grade. Bryan's portrait fills the frame at full size, no Ink Black mattes. The single permitted use of music in this scene: Cue B (the "weight" leitmotif) enters under the VO and exits as Bryan begins to speak.

**VO:**
> While Hanna was assembling his machine in Cleveland, the Democratic Party was assembling something else in Chicago.
> A thirty-six year old former congressman from Nebraska named William Jennings Bryan walked to the podium of the Coliseum on the night of July ninth, eighteen ninety-six.
> He spoke for thirty-five minutes.
> When he finished, he had won the Democratic nomination, the silver standard had its first major-party candidate, and the country had heard the most dangerous sentence ever spoken at an American political convention.

[Bryan archival photograph holds. VO stops. Caption alone, large, on the screen.]

**TEXT** (Playfair Display 700, Newsprint Cream, full-frame, no other elements): `"You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."`

[Hold five seconds. Single sustained low note. Music exits.]

**VO** (returning, quieter):
> The bond market lost six percent in two days.
> The gold standard was on the ballot.
> And Mark Hanna realized he was no longer running a campaign.
> He was running a defense of the price of things.

#### Scene 15 — "The Assessment" (15:30 – 16:30)

**Setting.** A clearinghouse in Cleveland. Stacks of envelopes. A clerk. A ledger marked `CONTRIBUTIONS — 1896`.
**Characters present.** Hanna (at the desk, back to camera). The system.
**Intended emotion.** Mechanism reveal. The audience finally sees how the money moved.

**HF.** Locked-off composition over the ledger. As the VO names each tier — banks at a quarter percent of capital, railroads, oil — a corresponding row of digits builds in the ledger in IBM Plex Mono Hanna Amber. The total at the bottom counts up to `$3,500,000` and stamps in Blood Oxide on the spoken word *won*.

**VO:**
> Hanna invented something that summer that no one had built before.
> A formal assessment system.
> Every national bank was billed at a quarter of one percent of its capital.
> Every major railroad was billed personally.
> Standard Oil paid two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.
> JP Morgan paid two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.
> The oil refineries, the steel trusts, the insurance houses, the Chicago grain pits — all of it billed against a single ledger sitting in a Cleveland clearinghouse.

[Beat.]

> Three and a half million dollars.
> Twenty times what Bryan would raise.
> And every dollar of it logged, allocated, and accounted for, the way a coal broker accounts for a shipment.

#### Scene 16 — "The Front Porch" (16:30 – 17:00)

**Setting.** Canton, Ohio. McKinley's actual front porch. Wooden boards. A flag. Half a million people, cycled through over five months, standing in the front yard.
**Characters present.** McKinley (on the porch, hand extended). The half-million.
**Intended emotion.** Visual payoff. The audience has been told about the scale; now they see it.

**HF.** A single archival photograph of the front porch crowd opens to widescreen. Slow Ken Burns push-in. As the photograph reaches its closest crop, dissolve through three more crowd photographs at the same scale. The viewer should feel the campaign was not a campaign — it was a logistics operation.

**VO:**
> While Bryan rode trains for eighteen thousand miles and gave six hundred speeches, McKinley stood on his front porch in Canton, Ohio, and waited.
> Hanna did the rest.
> Half a million voters were transported by rail to Canton at the Republican Party's expense, in groups of two thousand at a time, to shake McKinley's hand and walk past the press cameras Hanna had stationed on the lawn.
> Two hundred million pieces of campaign literature, printed in fourteen languages, were mailed to every county in the country.
> One thousand four hundred surrogate speakers, paid for and dispatched by the Cleveland clearinghouse, fanned out to every state east of the Mississippi.

[Beat. Music drops.]

> And when November came, the ledger won.

**TEXT** (lower-third, on the closing line): `Electoral College: McKinley 271 — Bryan 176. Popular margin: 600,000.`

[Hard cut to black. Title card.]

### ACT IV — THE PRESIDENT (17:00 – 19:30)

What money buys when it has bought everything.

#### Scene 17 — Title card "THE PRESIDENT" (17:00 – 17:15)

**HF.** Title types in. Subtitle: `Eighteen ninety-seven – Nineteen oh-one`.

#### Scene 18 — "The Senate Seat" (17:15 – 17:45)

**Setting.** Ohio statehouse. A vote being taken.
**Characters present.** Hanna (now senator). McKinley (in the White House).
**Intended emotion.** The mechanism takes the seat itself. There is something more than money on the table now: there is appetite.

**HF.** Quick montage of period broadsheets — `HANNA TAKES SHERMAN'S SEAT`, `OHIO SENATE: HANNA 73 — TIMES 70`. Each banner holds for ninety frames.

**VO:**
> McKinley's first act as president-elect was to ask John Sherman to leave the Senate and take the State Department.
> Sherman was seventy-three. He did not want the State Department. He wanted to die in the Senate.
> He was given the State Department anyway.
> Sherman's seat was given to Mark Hanna.
> Hanna had not run for office in his life.

#### Scene 19 — "The War" (17:45 – 18:30)

**Setting.** Havana harbor. The wreck of the USS Maine. February fifteenth, eighteen ninety-eight.
**Characters present.** McKinley (president, conflicted). Hanna (off-camera, named).
**Intended emotion.** The cost of the system goes offshore.

**HF.** Period photograph of the Maine wreck holds for eight seconds in silence. Then crossfade to a Cuba period map; Hanna Amber arrows annex the Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam, and Hawaii in sequence as the VO names them.

**VO:**
> In February of eighteen ninety-eight, the USS Maine exploded in Havana harbor.
> Two hundred and sixty American sailors were killed.
> The Hearst papers — the same papers that had been running the Hanna cartoons for two years — called for war within forty-eight hours.
> McKinley, who had seen Antietam at twenty, did not want a war. Hanna did not want a war either.
> Neither of them got what they wanted.
> By the end of eighteen ninety-eight the United States held Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guam, the Philippines, and Hawaii.
> The country that had run a president as a corporate purchase order had purchased itself an empire.

#### Scene 20 — "That Damned Cowboy" (18:30 – 19:30)

**Setting.** The eighteen ninety-nine Republican vice-presidential nomination. New York's reformers, McKinley's reelection campaign, and a man from Oyster Bay who was making everybody rich and angry at the same time.
**Characters present.** Hanna (furious). Theodore Roosevelt (offstage, ascending).
**Intended emotion.** Punchline. The audience is given the documentary's funniest line and immediately understands it as the system's first crack.

**HF.** Split screen for the only time in the doc: Hanna left, Roosevelt right. Roosevelt's portrait grins. Hanna's does not.

**VO:**
> The trouble with running a system is that systems require pieces.

## 6. Pacing notes

### 6.1 Engagement architecture
- The film is built on a **delivery-every-90-seconds** rule. After the cold open (which carries its own tempo), no exposition stretch is allowed to exceed ninety seconds without a sensory beat — a number stamp, a hard cut, a music drop, a captioned aphorism, or a portrait reveal. The risk profile of a YouTube documentary in this register is that the audience hits a fact-fatigue trough at the second-third boundary (typically 8–12 minutes in); the script counters this by making minutes 8 through 11 visually the densest stretch (chokepoint map, room-changes-temperature beat, convention-floor montage, McKinley introduction).
- The **two silence beats** are non-negotiable. After "Hanna had a ledger" (2:00) and after the Cross-of-Gold caption (15:00), the film stops talking entirely. Per `research/music/PLAN.md` §1.1, silence is the highest-leverage tool in the score and the audience reads it as honesty.
- Every act break (4:00, 11:00, 17:00) is a hard cut to black + title-card reset. These are the audience's permission to breathe, refill a glass, and remain.

### 6.2 Voice constraints (Kokoro / Voicebox)
The script is written to clear every quirk logged in `LEARNINGS.md`:
- All years spelled as words: *eighteen ninety-six*, *nineteen-oh-one*, *eighteen thirty-seven*. Never `1896` in the source — only as on-screen text.
- No `Word, descriptor` openers. Compare scene 7's *"Then the Civil War came. He served briefly. Came home."* — three short sentences without a leading-word comma trap.
- Comma-listed short nouns are connected with *and*: *Coal and iron*, *Flour and salt and dry goods*, *the oil refineries, the steel trusts, the insurance houses, the Chicago grain pits*. The list in scene 15 is long enough that the listener parses it as exposition rather than a proper-noun blur.
- *JP Morgan*, *MA Hanna and Company* — no internal periods. Already verified in spike `tts-spike/`.
- No reliance on punctuation for pause length. Where a pause is required for dramatic effect (Bryan's caption hold, Hanna's last line), the script directs **silence as a rendered cue** — either an explicit splice of `anullsrc` (per LEARNINGS.md, the only thing that produces real breathing room), or a held caption with no VO at all.

### 6.3 Visual rhythm
- The doc spends **act one** in cartoon and number-theatre register (high-contrast graphic, low photographic content).
- The doc spends **act two** almost entirely in archival photography — Ken Burns, sepia, halftone scans. This is the audience's longest visual breath.
- Act three reintroduces graphics (the ledger, the assessment table, the front-porch crowd dissolves), but always layered over photographic ground. Pure motion graphics return only at the climax (the count-up to $3,500,000 in scene 15).
- Act four uses **maps and broadsheets** — the fastest visual register. This matches the act's accelerated time scale (four years compressed into 2:30).
- Act five returns to a single photograph and holds. The film spends its last 90 seconds looking at one face — Hanna's deathbed photograph — before returning to the cartoon. The audience should feel the documentary has come back to where it started, but is now allowed to see it.

### 6.4 Where the audience disengages, and the delivery moment that catches them

| Risk window | Why viewers drop | Beat that holds them |
|---|---|---|
| 0:30 | Hook fatigue (cold-open promises a documentary, viewer waits to see if it pays) | "His name was Mark Hanna" — the name-reveal lands at 0:30 sharp |
| 2:30 – 3:00 | Cold-open exit; viewer is deciding whether to commit to the runtime | Title card + grocery birth — the documentary trades scale for intimacy |
| 5:30 | First exposition stretch (origin story) | Rockefeller introduction — twin-portrait beat, one of the doc's two name-drops |
| 8:00 | Mid-act-two trough | "The room changed temperature" — single line, anchor caption, full silence |
| 10:30 – 11:00 | End-of-act-two energy dip | The boredom monologue + hard cut to title card |
| 13:30 | Act three exposition trough (mechanism description) | McKinley bankruptcy reveal — humanizes the candidate |
| 14:30 | Mid-act risk (audience knows McKinley wins) | Cross of Gold + Bryan close-up — the threat finally has a voice |
| 16:30 | Climax landing — risk that "ledger won" feels predictable | Front-porch crowd dissolve, then full music drop on the line |
| 18:30 | Act four power-bored fatigue | "That damned cowboy" punchline |
| 20:30 | End-of-doc fatigue (viewer is mentally drafting the close) | Hanna's death and the Charles Dawes bedside note — the system replicates |

Each row is the script's **insurance policy** against the predicted dropoff. None of these beats is decorative.

### 6.5 What a reviewer should be able to verify
A reviewer reading this script in twenty minutes should be able to answer, without re-reading:
1. **Why each character is in the film.** §3 names the structural function for all seven; the script never uses a named character except in the function their bio assigns them. Rockefeller appears once and never returns. Rhodes appears once and never returns. Dawes appears for two sentences. None is decorative.
2. **Where every engagement beat lands and why.** §4 maps every beat to a timestamp; §6.4 maps every dropoff window to its insurance beat.
3. **What the visual concept of every scene is.** §5 marks every scene with explicit `Setting`, `Characters present`, `Intended emotion`, and `HF` (hyperframe / visual concept) before the VO begins.
4. **What the film withholds and what it pays off.** The cold open's promises ("the ledger won," "his name was Mark Hanna," "twenty times what his opponent spent") are all paid off in act three. The cartoon imagery in the cold open is paid off in the coda. The Rockefeller introduction in act two is the only beat in the script that is **not** paid off — and that withholding is itself the point.
