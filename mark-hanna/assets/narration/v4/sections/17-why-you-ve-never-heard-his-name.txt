Mark Hanna died in nineteen oh four. The Davenport image — Hanna in the dollar-sign suit, cigar the size of a forearm — kept circulating for years through reprints, Bryan campaign materials, and the eighteen ninety-eight collected edition Davenport published before moving from the Journal to the rival Evening Mail. By the early 1920s it was gone.

Three things erased him. They did it slowly. They did it without anyone noticing.

The first was Roosevelt's Tillman Act. After January nineteen oh seven Hanna's methods were a federal crime. No Republican Party leader could celebrate the man who invented the practice the party's own president had just outlawed. Hanna's name became politically toxic to the people who had benefited most from his work. The party that owed him everything had every reason to stop mentioning him. So it stopped.

The second was the textbook revisions across the interwar decades. American high schools standardized their history curricula in the years after the First World War. The Gilded Age chapter tightened. The Progressive Era expanded. The story became Roosevelt versus the trusts, Wilson versus the banks, the muckrakers versus the bosses.

Hanna did not fit. He was not a clean villain. McKinley's family liked him. The press corps respected him. His Senate colleagues mourned him.

In his last years, he co-founded the National Civic Federation, an experiment in putting corporate executives and union leaders at the same table. He was the only Gilded Age industrialist who would publicly share a stage with the head of the largest American union of the era.

And he was not a hero either. He was the mechanism. By the 1930s the new textbooks had dropped him. A generation of American students learned the eighteen ninety-six campaign without learning the name of the man who ran it.

The third is the one you can measure in twenty twenty-six. Hanna's Wikipedia article averages around six thousand views a month. McKinley's averages a hundred and forty-five thousand. Theodore Roosevelt's averages three hundred and twenty-four thousand. John D. Rockefeller's, the man who paid Hanna's first assessment in eighteen ninety-six, averages a hundred and fifty-one thousand.

Hanna gets one fiftieth of Roosevelt's traffic. He gets one twenty-fifth of Rockefeller's. The other names from the eighteen ninety-six campaign are fifty times more interesting to the present-day reader than the man who built the campaign.

Americans search for what Hanna built. They do not search for him. The erasure that started with Roosevelt's reforms and continued through the textbook revisions of the interwar decades is still active. It is still working. The reason you have never heard his name is that for one hundred and twenty years the people who knew it had reasons not to say it.

In eighteen ninety-six the cartoon was a joke. Mark Hanna in a suit covered in dollar signs, smoking a cigar the size of a forearm — every week, every Hearst paper, the same man. The joke was that anyone could see what Hanna was doing.

The joke was right.

He raised the money. He elected the president. He invented modern campaign finance. The system the cartoon was mocking became the way American elections work. The cartoon stopped running. The system did not.

This is the man nobody remembers.
