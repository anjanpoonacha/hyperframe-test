"Don't any of you realize there's only one life between this madman and the presidency?"

Mark Hanna said it on the convention floor in Philadelphia, June twenty-first, nineteen hundred. He said it loud enough to be heard across the room. Reporters quoted it that afternoon. By the end of the week the line had reached every major paper in the country.

The madman in question was Theodore Roosevelt.

The trouble had started in eighteen ninety-nine. Vice President Garret Hobart — Hanna's closest ally inside the McKinley administration, the man Hanna had personally selected for the eighteen ninety-six ticket — fell ill in the autumn and died on November twenty-first, eighteen ninety-nine. McKinley was seeking a second term. The vice presidency was open.

The New York Republican machine, run by Senator Thomas Platt, had been trying for two years to dispose of the state's reform governor: a forty-one-year-old reformer named Theodore Roosevelt. Rough Rider in the Spanish-American War, former Police Commissioner of New York City, the loudest voice for civil-service reform in the Republican Party.

Roosevelt had spent his term breaking up the corrupt patronage system that kept Platt's organization alive. Platt wanted him gone. The vice presidency was a gilded coffin. Platt began maneuvering to make it Roosevelt's.

Hanna was horrified. He had spent four years building a relationship with McKinley that defined American politics. The vice president was one bullet from the presidency. And Hanna did not trust Roosevelt — not because Roosevelt was a bad man, but because Roosevelt was a moralist. Hanna's politics were transactional. Roosevelt did not do deals. He picked fights and refused to fund them. He would inherit the corporate alliances that funded the party — and break them.

The Republican National Convention opened in Philadelphia on June nineteenth, nineteen hundred. Roosevelt arrived as the Western delegations' favorite. Platt had already pledged New York. Pennsylvania followed. By the second day Roosevelt had momentum. The nomination was going to happen.

Hanna stood on the Philadelphia floor watching the votes accumulate. He had built the Republican Party's machinery. He chaired its committee. And he could not stop the convention from nominating, for vice president of the United States, a man he believed would destroy everything the party stood for.

That was when he said it. The line that would follow him for the rest of his life.

The convention nominated Roosevelt for vice president on June twenty-first, nineteen hundred. McKinley accepted. Hanna campaigned for the ticket anyway. McKinley and Roosevelt won the November election by a wider margin than McKinley and Hobart had won in eighteen ninety-six.

Hanna was right. There was only one life between Roosevelt and the presidency. It was William McKinley's.
