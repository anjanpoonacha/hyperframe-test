On January twenty-first, twenty ten, the Supreme Court of the United States decided Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission by a vote of five to four. The opinion held that corporate political expenditures, when made independently of a candidate's campaign, are protected speech under the First Amendment.

The Tillman Act's federal ban on corporate political spending was effectively gutted. The wall Roosevelt had built around Hanna's system in nineteen oh seven came down in twenty ten. Within five years of the Citizens United decision, independent corporate political spending exceeded one billion dollars per election cycle. By twenty twenty-four, corporate-funded Super PACs had raised more than four billion dollars in a single election.

Hanna's system was back. In a different legal form. But back.

The court did not cite Mark Hanna. The plaintiffs did not invoke him. The dissents did not name him. Hanna had been dead for one hundred and six years, and the system he built was being relegalized in a courtroom four blocks from the Senate floor where he had served his only term. The country had forgotten the man whose machinery it was rebuilding.
