In early February nineteen oh four Mark Hanna fell ill at the Arlington Hotel in Washington, D.C. — two blocks from the White House, the city's most fashionable address, the place where senators stayed when they did not keep a Washington home.

He was running a fever. The Senate was in session. He was working from his hotel room. The diagnosis came back: typhoid fever. A disease of contaminated water and poor sanitation, common in the city's poorer neighborhoods, less common in its hotels.

The Theodore Roosevelt Center holds the medical bulletin. It is dated February twelfth, nineteen oh four. The bulletin reports active illness. By February fourteenth he could not sit up.

Hanna died at six forty in the evening on February fifteenth, nineteen oh four.

He was sixty-six years old. He had served in the United States Senate for six years and eleven months. He had chaired the Republican National Committee for eight. He had run a Cleveland business for forty.

His body was returned to Cleveland by funeral train. He was buried at Lake View Cemetery, on the same hilltop where he had helped arrange the burial of James Garfield twenty-three years earlier. Twenty thousand people attended.

Roosevelt did not come. He sent his Cabinet.

Four days after the funeral, on February nineteenth, nineteen oh four, Theodore Roosevelt wrote a letter to his son Ted at Groton:

"Poor Hanna's death was a tragedy. At the end he wrote me a note, the last he ever wrote, which showed him at his best, and which I much appreciate. His death was very sad for his family and close friends, for he had many large and generous traits, and had made a great success in life by his energy, perseverance and burly strength."

Roosevelt could afford to be generous. He had won.
