Hanna was sixty-four years old when McKinley died. He had two and a half years left, though no one knew that yet. He spent them in a slow-motion confrontation with the man he had not been able to stop in Philadelphia.

Roosevelt and Hanna agreed on almost nothing. Roosevelt wanted to break up the trusts. Hanna had built his fortune on them. Roosevelt wanted to regulate the railroads. Hanna sat on the boards of three of them. Roosevelt wanted to give labor a seat at the table. Hanna had spent forty years keeping it off. The two men shared a party, a Cabinet, and almost nothing else.

In the autumn of nineteen oh three, with the nineteen oh four nomination a year away, Hanna began quietly weighing whether to challenge Roosevelt for the Republican nomination. He never said so publicly. He never built an organization. He told confidants he was thinking about it. He let the corporate donors who had funded McKinley know he was available. He did not announce.

Roosevelt and his ally Joseph Foraker — two-term Ohio governor, Senator since eighteen ninety-seven, the man Hanna had blocked from a Cabinet seat — knew. They set a trap.

The Ohio Republican State Convention met in Columbus in October nineteen oh three. A resolution was introduced from the floor endorsing President Roosevelt's renomination in nineteen oh four. The resolution was a year early. State conventions did not normally endorse presidential nominees until the spring of an election year. The timing was deliberate. It was meant to force Hanna's hand.

Hanna did what any operator of his experience would do: he went to Roosevelt directly. He told the President he might oppose the resolution as premature. He framed it as a procedural objection, not a political one. He gave Roosevelt the chance to back off.

Roosevelt's reply, sent by letter, was one of the most elegant political knives in American history. He was not requesting Hanna's support, he wrote. He was not requesting anyone's support. But he was confident that all loyal supporters of his administration would naturally vote for the resolution. The decision, of course, was Hanna's.

Voting against the resolution would have been a public answer to the question.

Hanna voted yes. He had no choice. The Ohio convention passed the endorsement unanimously. The nineteen oh four nomination was settled. Hanna's challenge ended before it began.

He had been outplayed by a man he had publicly called a madman. Outplayed without a single dollar being raised, a single delegate being courted, or a single overt threat being made.

Four months later he was dead.
