In January eighteen ninety-five Hanna resigned from active management of MA Hanna and Company. He was fifty-seven years old. He turned the iron and coal operation over to his brother and his cousins. He took his name off the door in everything but title. He walked away from business.

He moved to New York City and rented offices in Manhattan. He set up a second headquarters in Chicago, under a young Ohio attorney named Charles Dawes. Dawes ran the field operation. Hanna ran the money.

He began making lists. Lists of bankers in New York. Lists of railroad presidents in Chicago. Lists of corporation heads from Pittsburgh to Saint Louis. He began making appointments. He began calling on people whose names had never appeared on a campaign ledger before.

He was building, in a Wall Street office at fifty-seven years old, the network he had wanted at thirty-five and had never been in a position to construct. He had four years to do it. He believed he had three.

The Republican National Convention opened in Saint Louis on June sixteenth, eighteen ninety-six. Two days later, on the first ballot, the convention nominated William McKinley for President of the United States. The vote was six hundred and sixty-one to 84. McKinley had been a candidate on paper for eleven days. He had been Hanna's candidate for eight years.

The Republican National Committee elected Hanna its Chairman on June 19. He now controlled every dollar the campaign would raise or spend between June and November. He had four months. He had a country to organize. And he had an idea about how to do it that no one had ever tried at scale.
