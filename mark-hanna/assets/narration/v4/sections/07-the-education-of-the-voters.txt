Hanna called the campaign an education.

Educating seventy-six million Americans in four months means industrial scale.

The Republican National Committee printed two hundred million pamphlets in the four months between June and November. Two hundred million. The American population in eighteen ninety-six was seventy-six million. Hanna mailed every adult voter the equivalent of four pamphlets each.

The literature was multilingual. He printed in German for the Pennsylvania and Wisconsin farmers. He printed in Yiddish for the New York garment workers. He printed in Spanish for the Southwest. He printed in Italian for the eastern cities. He printed in Polish, Czech, Swedish, and Norwegian for the upper Midwest.

The English-language pamphlets came in dozens of variants. One for railway workers. One for factory hands. One for shopkeepers. One for farmers in silver-mining states, and a different one for farmers in gold-standard states. The campaign tracked which version was sent to which county.

Hanna deployed fourteen hundred surrogate speakers. He paid their travel. He coordinated their schedules. He kept them on message. Every county in every battleground state heard a Republican voice in person before the November vote.

The candidate stayed home. McKinley refused to travel. He believed a presidential nominee should not beg for votes on a railroad platform. He told Hanna he would not campaign in the field. So Hanna brought the field to him.

The Pennsylvania Railroad, the Baltimore and Ohio, and a dozen other lines offered special excursion fares to Canton, Ohio — population thirty thousand, roughly three hundred miles from Washington, McKinley's home town since the Civil War. Voters arrived from as far as Maine and Nebraska. They walked from the train station to the candidate's front porch. McKinley spoke from his lawn. He shook every hand.

By Election Day, three quarters of a million Americans had stood in McKinley's front yard.

Bryan, meanwhile, traveled. The "Cross of Gold" speech he had delivered at the Democratic convention in Chicago on July ninth, eighteen ninety-six had made him the youngest major-party nominee in American history. The speech demanded the free coinage of silver to break what Bryan called the grip of Eastern banks on Western farmers. He closed it on a thesis: "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." The convention floor went silent for five seconds, then erupted.

He spent the next four months on trains. He gave six hundred speeches in twenty-seven states. He was magnificent in person. He was thirty-six years old and could fill a stadium with his voice alone.

Hanna's response was tonnage, not eloquence. While Bryan thundered to crowds of ten thousand at a time, Hanna delivered two hundred million pieces of mail. While Bryan gave six hundred speeches, Hanna deployed fourteen hundred speakers. While Bryan held one rally per town, Hanna held one in every precinct. The contest was not between two campaigners. It was between a man and a machine.

On November third the country would tell them which it preferred.
