The cartoon ran in every Hearst paper in eighteen ninety-six. A fat man in a suit covered in dollar signs, smoking a cigar the size of his forearm. The caption changed every week. The man did not.

His name was Mark Hanna.

He built the machine that bought American elections.

Mark Hanna was a Cleveland iron-and-coal millionaire. Fifty-nine years old. Had never run for office in his life.

It was the first campaign bought with corporate money at industrial scale.

Hanna raised three and a half million dollars that year. He spent twenty times what his opponent spent. He elected William McKinley, a wounded Civil War major and two-term Ohio governor, president of the United States. Banks paid. Railroads paid. Mark Hanna ran every dollar of it from a rented Manhattan office.

It was bought.

Standard Oil paid first. Carnegie Steel paid second. Banks and insurance companies paid in proportion to their capital. Hanna did not ask. He sent letters telling firms what they owed. No political consultant in American history has spent more, recruited more, or won bigger. Every campaign after eighteen ninety-six copied his playbook.

This is the man who built the operating system of American elections. This is the man nobody remembers.

Eight years later he was dead at sixty-six. The textbook revisions of the interwar decades took his name out of the standard curriculum. Today his Wikipedia article averages around six thousand views a month. Theodore Roosevelt's averages over three hundred thousand.

How one man bought a presidency. How the country he built forgot his name.
