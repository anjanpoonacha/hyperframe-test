The cartoon ran in every Hearst paper in America.

A fat man, in a suit covered in dollar signs, smoking a cigar the size of his forearm.

The caption changed every week. The man did not.

His name was Mark Hanna.

And in eighteen ninety-six, he did something no one had ever done at that scale.

He turned the American presidential election into a corporate purchase order.

Three and a half million dollars raised. Twenty times what his opponent spent.

Banks assessed at a quarter of a percent of capital. Railroads, shaken down personally. Standard Oil, two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. JP Morgan, two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.

Half a million voters, transported by train, to shake one man's hand.

Two hundred million pieces of campaign literature, printed and mailed.

The opposition had a thirty-six year old preacher who could move a crowd to tears.

Hanna had a ledger.

And when November came, the ledger won.

He wasn't born with money. He was born in a grocery.

New Lisbon, Ohio. Eighteen thirty-seven.

His father, Doctor Leonard Hanna, was a country physician who ran a wholesale grocery on the side. Flour, salt, dry goods to Ohio farmers.

When Mark was fifteen, the family moved to Cleveland. He enrolled at Western Reserve College.

In his class, was a quiet, awkward boy who would grow up to control ninety percent of the oil refining capacity in the United States.

His name was John D. Rockefeller.

The two would orbit each other for the next forty years.

Hanna lasted one year at Western Reserve before he quit. Not because he was failing. Because he had already figured out something the professors couldn't teach. The real education, was in the price of things.

He went back to the store. Worked the floor. Watched how men negotiated. Watched who paid on time and who didn't. Watched which suppliers had leverage, and which ones begged.

Then the Civil War came. He served briefly. Came home.

And married a woman named Charlotte Rhodes.

Her father was Daniel Rhodes. Coal and iron. One of the men quietly getting rich off everything Cleveland was becoming.

Hanna walked into that business, and never walked out.

By eighteen sixty-seven, Marcus Alonzo Hanna had built MA Hanna and Company. A coal and iron brokerage, sitting at the exact pivot point where Lake Erie ore boats unloaded for the Pittsburgh steel mills.

Cleveland was the chokepoint. And Hanna sat on it.

Every ton of iron ore that crossed Lake Erie in the eighteen seventies, passed through brokers like him, he took a cut.

Then he bought the newspaper, the Cleveland Herald. Then a theater. Then banking interests. Then a streetcar line.

By eighteen eighty, he controlled enough of Cleveland that when he walked into a room, the room changed temperature.

And he was bored. Not with money, he had money. Bored with the ceiling.

Because Hanna had figured out, watching the men who supplied his coal, the politicians who regulated his railroads, the judges who settled his contracts, that money was the second most powerful thing in America.

The most powerful thing, was who decided where the money went.

That was politics. And no one had ever run it like a business. Not yet.

The year was eighteen eighty-eight. The Republican National Convention. Chicago.

Mark Hanna arrived not as a candidate, but as a kingmaker.

His man was John Sherman. Ohio senator. Treasury veteran. The establishment choice.

Hanna worked the convention floor for six days. Trading delegates. Promising postmasterships. Calling in markers from Ohio to Pennsylvania.

Sherman peaked on the first ballot at two hundred and twenty-nine votes. Then he bled out. Ballot after ballot, until the nomination went to Benjamin Harrison on the eighth.

Hanna lost.

But he saw something that week he would never forget.

A forty-five year old congressman from Canton, Ohio had been chairing the resolutions committee.

When delegates began breaking toward him as a dark-horse alternative, the congressman walked to the microphone, and refused.

He told the convention he was bound to Sherman. And he sat down.

That kind of loyalty was rare in eighteen eighty-eight politics. It was almost extinct.

His name was William McKinley.

Hanna walked across the floor, and introduced himself.

He didn't know it yet. But he had just found his president.
