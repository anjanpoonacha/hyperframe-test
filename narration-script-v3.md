## SCENE 01 — COLD OPEN

The cartoon ran in every Hearst paper in America.

A fat man... in a suit covered in dollar signs... smoking a cigar the size of his forearm.

The caption changed every week.

The man did not.

His name was Mark Hanna.

And in 1896... he did something no one had ever done at that scale.

He turned the American presidential election... into a corporate purchase order.

$3.5 million raised. 20 times what his opponent spent.

Banks assessed at a quarter of a percent of capital.
Railroads shaken down personally.
Standard Oil — $250,000.
J.P. Morgan — $250,000.

Half a million voters... transported by train to shake one man's hand.

200 million pieces of campaign literature... printed and mailed.

The opposition had a 36-year-old preacher who could move a crowd to tears.

Hanna had a ledger.

And when November came...

...the ledger won.

---

## SCENE 02 — WHO WAS THIS MAN

He wasn't born with money.

He was born in a grocery.

New Lisbon, Ohio. 1837.

His father, Dr. Leonard Hanna, was a country physician who ran a wholesale grocery on the side — flour, salt, dry goods to Ohio farmers.

When Mark was fifteen, the family moved to Cleveland.

He enrolled at Western Reserve College.

In his class... was a quiet, awkward boy who would grow up to control 90 percent of the oil refining capacity in the United States.

His name was John D. Rockefeller.

The two would orbit each other for the next forty years.

Hanna lasted one year at Western Reserve before he quit.

Not because he was failing.

Because he had already figured out something the professors couldn't teach:

The real education... was in the price of things.

He went back to the store. Worked the floor. Watched how men negotiated. Watched who paid on time and who didn't. Watched which suppliers had leverage... and which ones begged.

Then the Civil War came.

He served briefly. Came home.

And married a woman named Charlotte Rhodes.

Her father was Daniel Rhodes — coal, iron, one of the men quietly getting rich off everything Cleveland was becoming.

Hanna walked into that business...

...and never walked out.

---

## SCENE 03 — THE MACHINE BEHIND THE MAN

By 1867, Marcus Alonzo Hanna had built M.A. Hanna & Co.

A coal and iron brokerage... sitting at the exact pivot point... where Lake Erie ore boats unloaded for the Pittsburgh steel mills.

Cleveland was the chokepoint.

And Hanna sat on it.

Every ton of iron ore that crossed Lake Erie in the 1870s... passed through brokers like him.

He took a cut.

Then he bought the newspaper — the Cleveland Herald.

Then a theater.

Then banking interests.

Then a streetcar line.

By 1880... he controlled enough of Cleveland that when he walked into a room, the room changed temperature.

And he was bored.

Not with money. He had money.

Bored with the ceiling.

Because Hanna had figured out — watching the men who supplied his coal, the politicians who regulated his railroads, the judges who settled his contracts — that money was the second-most powerful thing in America.

The most powerful thing...

...was who decided where the money went.

That was politics.

And no one had ever run it like a business.

Not yet.

---

## SCENE 04 — FINDING McKINLEY

The year was 1888.

The Republican National Convention. Chicago.

Mark Hanna arrived not as a candidate — but as a kingmaker.

His man was John Sherman. Ohio senator. Treasury veteran. The establishment choice.

Hanna worked the convention floor for six days... trading delegates... promising postmasterships... calling in markers from Ohio to Pennsylvania.

Sherman peaked on the first ballot at 229 votes.

Then he bled out.

Ballot after ballot... until the nomination went to Benjamin Harrison on the eighth.

Hanna lost.

But he saw something that week he would never forget.

A 45-year-old congressman from Canton, Ohio had been chairing the resolutions committee.

When delegates began breaking toward HIM as a dark-horse alternative... the congressman walked to the microphone... and refused.

He told the convention he was bound to Sherman.

And he sat down.

That kind of loyalty was rare in 1888 politics.

It was almost extinct.

His name was William McKinley.

Hanna walked across the floor... and introduced himself.

He didn't know it yet.

But he had just found his president.

---

## SCENE 05 — THE EDUCATION OF A CAMPAIGN

Over the next seven years, Hanna built McKinley like a product.

He managed his run for Ohio governor in 1891. Victory.
Then again in 1893. Another victory.

But the real moment — the moment that bound them together for life — came in February of 1893.

McKinley got wiped out.

A friend of his named Robert Walker — a tin-plate manufacturer in Youngstown — collapsed in the Panic of 1893.

McKinley had co-signed his loans.

Quietly. As a favor.

When Walker went under... the debt landed on the governor of Ohio.

The number was staggering.

One hundred and thirty thousand dollars.

In 1893 money — that was a fortune. Enough to ruin a man for life.

McKinley offered to resign the governorship.

Hanna told him no.

Then Hanna picked up the phone.

Myron Herrick. H.H. Kohlsaat. Andrew Carnegie.

The Cleveland steel money. The Chicago newspaper money. The Pittsburgh iron money.

Within weeks... the debt was gone.

No press. No paper trail. No fingerprints.

And William McKinley owed Mark Hanna...

...everything.

That is what most political operators never learn.

You don't just own a candidate's future.

You own their loyalty... by owning their past.

By 1895, the product was finished.

All Hanna needed now... was a buyer big enough to matter.

He picked the biggest one in the country.

The White House.

---

## SCENE 06 — THE OPERATION

Hanna resigned from his businesses.

Completely.

He became, full-time, the campaign manager for William McKinley.

He moved to New York. Set up offices at the Fifth Avenue Hotel.

The treasurer of the Republican National Committee was a New York banker named Cornelius Bliss.

Hanna and Bliss started making calls.

The system they built was called the assessment.

Every major corporation in America received a number.

Not a request. Not a suggestion.

A number.

Banks: one quarter of one percent of every dollar on their books.

Standard Oil: $250,000.
J.P. Morgan's banking house: $250,000.

If you paid — you went in the ledger.
And when your industry needed a friend in the Senate... a favorable tariff... a favorable judge...
you would find one.

If you refused...
your name went on a different list.

The total haul: $3.5 million.

By comparison — William Jennings Bryan, the Democratic nominee, raised roughly $300,000.

That is not a close race.

That is a buyout.

---

## SCENE 07 — THE MAN ON THE OTHER SIDE

But before we talk about the machine...

...you have to understand what it was built to stop.

July 9, 1896. Chicago. The Democratic National Convention.

The Democratic Party was looking for a candidate.

What they had... was a 36-year-old former congressman from Nebraska who had lost his Senate race two years earlier.

A has-been.

Most delegates didn't know his name.

He walked to the podium anyway.

He spoke for 20 minutes.

No notes.

At the end, he spread his arms wide... and said:

"You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns.
You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

The convention hall went silent.

For five seconds, nobody moved.

Then... it erupted.

William Jennings Bryan was nominated on the fifth ballot.

He was the most dangerous opponent Hanna had ever faced.

Not because of money.

Because of truth.

Bryan was speaking for tens of millions of Americans who had nothing left to lose —
the farmers drowning in debt, the workers watching their wages collapse,
the people who had been told for two decades that the gold standard was economic law,
and that questioning it made you a radical.

Bryan said it was a lie.

And nearly half the country believed him.

---

## SCENE 08 — THE STRATEGY

Hanna looked at Bryan's campaign... and made a cold calculation.

Bryan was going to fight with fire.

Hanna would fight with paper.

While Bryan traveled 18,000 miles by train and gave 600 speeches...

McKinley never left his front porch in Canton, Ohio.

That was not laziness.

That was Hanna's design.

Hanna transported the voters to the candidate.

Train fare: paid by the campaign.

500,000 people... traveled to Canton... to shake McKinley's hand... on his front porch... in organized delegations... in a single campaign season.

Each group got a carefully rehearsed speech from McKinley.

Each group left with literature.

The literature operation was the real weapon.

Hanna's "literary bureau" — run by an operative named Perry Heath — printed 200 million pieces of campaign material.

Not a typo.

200 million.

Pamphlets in German. Polish. Italian. Yiddish. Swedish.
A dozen languages, targeting the immigrant factory wards Bryan was trying to reach.

The argument was brutally simple.

You speak to a German steelworker in Pittsburgh...

...in German...

...and you tell him:

Bryan wants to inflate the dollar.
The dollars in your pocket — what you've saved for your family — will buy less bread next month than they do this month.
You think you're voting for the little guy.
You're voting for the man who will make you poorer.

It worked.

The Republican National Committee dispatched 1,400 paid speakers across the country.

Bryan had a voice.

Hanna had a distribution network.

---

## SCENE 09 — ELECTION NIGHT

November 3, 1896. Canton, Ohio.

The returns came in by telegraph all night.

McKinley sat in his front parlor.

Hanna sat next to him.

By midnight... it was over.

Two hundred seventy-one electoral votes.
A six hundred thousand vote popular margin.
The first decisive Republican win in twenty years.

The industrial cities. The immigrant wards. The factory towns Hanna had blanketed in 14 languages — they all came in red.

Bryan swept the South. The plains. The mountain West.

McKinley swept everywhere else.

And when the final wires confirmed it...

...the most powerful political operator in America...

...put his face in his hands...

...and cried.

The grocer's son from Ohio had just purchased the presidency of the United States.

---

## SCENE 10 — SENATOR HANNA

McKinley needed to repay him.

So he made an opening.

John Sherman — the same Senator Hanna had failed to nominate eight years earlier — was promoted to Secretary of State.

It freed up Sherman's Senate seat.

The trade was almost too obvious.

Ohio's governor, Asa Bushnell, was a Hanna rival — and he didn't want to make the appointment.

But the pressure from Washington was overwhelming.

Bushnell folded.

In March 1897, he appointed Mark Hanna to the United States Senate.

One year later... Hanna won the seat outright.

The kingmaker was now a king.

For the next six years, he ran the Senate Republican caucus the same way he ran campaigns.

Systematically. Transactionally.

He was not beloved.

He was not meant to be.

He was effective.

And while he was effective...

...he was also quietly terrified of one thing.

---

## SCENE 11 — THE MADMAN

Theodore Roosevelt.

Governor of New York. War hero. Reform politician.

And a man that every New York machine boss desperately wanted to get rid of.

The 1900 Republican convention presented the perfect solution: elevate Roosevelt to the vice presidency.

Kick him upstairs. Neutralize him.

Hanna saw it immediately.

He stood up in a private meeting and said — in front of the delegates:

"Don't any of you realize there's only one life between that madman and the Presidency?"

Nobody listened.

Roosevelt became vice president.

And on September 6, 1901...

...at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York...

...an anarchist stepped out of a receiving line.

His name was Leon Czolgosz. (CHOL-gosh)

He fired two shots into William McKinley's chest.

McKinley died eight days later.

When the news reached Mark Hanna...

He said:

"Now that damned cowboy is president of the United States."

---

## SCENE 12 — THE TRAP SPRINGS

Hanna had worked his entire career to control the White House.

He had bought it. Managed it. Staffed it.

And in one act of political theater — by the bosses he thought were his allies — he had handed it to the one man he couldn't control.

Theodore Roosevelt was 42 years old.

The youngest president in American history.

And he had absolutely no interest in Mark Hanna's ledger.

Roosevelt began dismantling the machine almost immediately.

Antitrust suits. Railroad regulation. Conservation policy.

Everything Hanna had built his alliances around — the corporations, the trusts, the railroad men — found themselves suddenly on the wrong side of the White House.

Hanna began organizing a challenge.

He was going to primary Roosevelt in 1904.

Use the same machine.

Raise the same money.

And take back what he'd built.

---

## SCENE 13 — THE END

In the winter of 1904, Mark Hanna was still dangerous.

He had the Ohio machine. He had the donor network. He had IOUs going back twenty years.

Roosevelt knew it.

The White House started working the same levers Hanna had always worked.

Roosevelt cultivated Joseph Foraker — Hanna's Ohio rival in the Senate.

He made federal appointments that bled Hanna's patronage.

He pressured state party leaders... reminded senators which side of history they wanted to be on.

Ohio was slipping out from under Hanna for the first time in twenty years.

Then his body failed him.

In January 1904, he checked into the Arlington Hotel in Washington, D.C.

He had typhoid fever.

He was still writing letters from the bed.
Still sending telegrams.
Still working the phones.
Still certain he had time.

He didn't.

February 15, 1904.

Mark Hanna was dead at 66.

He never got his rematch.

The most powerful political operative in America — the man who had personally purchased a presidency, built a national party machine, and made corporate campaign finance the operating system of American democracy —

died in a hotel room.

Losing.

---

## SCENE 14 — WHAT CAME AFTER

Three years after Hanna died...

Congress passed the Tillman Act of 1907.

Named for Senator "Pitchfork Ben" Tillman — a populist from South Carolina who had been watching Mark Hanna's operation for a decade.

The Tillman Act banned corporations from making direct financial contributions to federal election campaigns.

For the first time in American history.

A $5,000 fine. Up to one year in prison.

It was the law that Mark Hanna had made necessary.

It would stand for over a hundred years.

Reinforced. Amended. Watered down.

But standing.

Until...

January 21, 2010.

Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission.

The Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4:

Corporations have First Amendment rights.
Limiting their political spending is unconstitutional.

In the dissent, Justice John Paul Stevens wrote:

"The Court's ruling threatens to undermine the integrity of elected institutions across the Nation."

Mark Hanna had been dead for 106 years.

His system was back.

---

## SCENE 15 — WHY HE WAS FORGOTTEN

So why don't most people know this man?

A hundred years after Hanna died... a young Texas operative named Karl Rove kept a portrait on his White House office wall.

It wasn't of Reagan.

It wasn't of Lincoln.

It was William McKinley.

Rove called the 2000 election... a "realigning election."

The exact phrase historians use for 1896.

He told reporters he wanted to build a permanent Republican majority.

The same goal Hanna had stated in 1895.

When the parallel got too obvious... Rove stopped giving interviews about Hanna.

But the portrait stayed on the wall.

Because here is the secret of how power actually moves in America:

The fighter gets remembered.

The plumber doesn't.

Theodore Roosevelt became the hero of the progressive era — the trust-buster, the conservationist, the rough rider.

History loves a fighter.

History is much less interested in the man who built the thing the fighter was fighting.

Mark Hanna was the backstory.

The infrastructure.

The machine.

And machines, by design... are supposed to be invisible.

There is one other reason.

Homer Davenport drew Mark Hanna for William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal — every single week — for eight years straight.

The same image, week after week.

Fat man. Dollar signs. Cigar.

McKinley dangling from his finger like a puppet.

Hundreds of papers carried it. Millions of readers saw it. Eight years of repetition.

By the time Hanna died... the cartoon WAS the man.

A caricature replaced a biography.

And the real story...

...that one man had figured out how to buy an election...

...wholesale...

...got lost inside the joke.

The less you understand how a machine works...

...the harder it is to dismantle it.

---

## SCENE 16 — CLOSING

"There are two things that are important in politics.

The first is money.

And I can't remember what the second one is."

The line is attributed to Mark Hanna.

He was joking.

Mostly.

He built the modern American campaign finance system.

Not as a theory.

As a working prototype.

He proved it could be done — that an election could be industrialized, that corporate money could be organized and deployed as systematically as a railroad schedule, that the candidate didn't need to be brilliant...

...he just needed to be manageable.

Mark Hanna died in February 1904.

The system he built...

...is still running.

It's running right now.

It never stopped.
